The True Spiritual Roots of Gonja: Why the Kamara Stand First

September 3, 2026

The history of Islam in Gonja begins with the clerics who stood beside Ndewura Jakpa when the Gonja Kingdom was being formed.

Among those clerics were the ancestors of the Kamara of Larabanga and Dakurpe.

They were not simply Muslims who settled in Gonja after the kingdom had been established. They were brought into the work of Ndewura Jakpa as spiritual guides. They accompanied him and his warriors during the conquests, offered prayers and spiritual assistance, and played a role in the religious life of the emerging kingdom.

This is why the place of the Kamara in Gonja Islamic history is older than the later question of who held which formal position at the royal court.

The Kamara were the clerics.

And they were there from the beginning.

The Coming of the Clerics

According to the historical tradition preserved in Larabanga and Dakurpe, Ndewura Jakpa sought the assistance of a respected Muslim cleric, Ayuba Lansori, who was associated with Medina.

Ayuba was already old and could not make the journey himself. He therefore sent trusted men, among them Yidan Braimah and his elder brother Kabiru, also known as Dokurugu or Dabkurugu.

Their purpose was clear: to provide Jakpa with spiritual guidance.

They did not arrive merely to establish a settlement. They came in connection with Jakpa and his campaigns. They prayed for him, guided him and accompanied his warriors.

The distinction matters.

Ndewura Jakpa was building a kingdom through conquest. His warriors fought the battles, but the religious side of those campaigns was entrusted to clerics.

The Kamara occupied that position.

Kongote and the Conquest of Kango

One of the clearest memories of this relationship survives in the name Kongote.

The Kamara tradition connects the appellation with the conquest of Kango, an ancient town in what is now Côte d'Ivoire. According to the tradition, Jakpa had encountered difficulty in conquering Kango and sought the spiritual assistance of the Kamara clerics.

Published accounts of Larabanga preserve this tradition and explain Kongote as a reference to the end of Kango and to the Kamara role in the conquest. The greeting has continued in the relationship between Gonjas and Kamara.

That is more than an interesting name.

It is a surviving reminder of the relationship between the Gonja warriors and their clerics.

The Kamara were not spectators to the expansion of the kingdom. Their spiritual responsibilities travelled with the warriors.

This is why reducing their early role to simply “teaching Islam” misses an important part of the history.

They guided.

They prayed.

They advised.

And they stood alongside Jakpa during the difficult work of establishing the Gonja Kingdom.

The Kamara Came Before the Later Court Arrangement

This is also where the distinction between the Kamara and the Sakpari becomes important.

The two should not be placed at the same point in history simply because both eventually became associated with Islamic leadership in Gonja.

The Kamara were already established as clerics around Jakpa.

The tradition preserved in Larabanga and Dakurpe further holds that one of those who received Islamic instruction from the early Kamara clerics later became associated with the Sakpari line and was directed to remain with Jakpa and pray for him.

If that tradition is followed to its logical conclusion, the chronology is straightforward.

The Kamara did not receive their religious position from the Sakpari.

The Kamara were already clerics.

The later Sakpari connection to Jakpa developed within a religious environment in which the Kamara were already present.

That distinction cannot be reversed simply because the Sakpari later became closely associated with the royal court.

The Place Given to the Kamara

The relationship between Ndewura Jakpa and the Kamara did not end with the conquests.

Yidan Braimah is associated with Larabanga, while his elder brother Kabiru is associated with Dakurpe. These became enduring Kamara communities and important centres of Islamic life.

That arrangement is significant.

Jakpa did not simply absorb the clerics into his palace and make them ordinary members of the royal establishment. The Kamara were given places where their own religious communities could develop.

Larabanga, in particular, became a major centre of Islamic life.

The Larabanga Mosque is regarded by the Ghana Museums and Monuments Board as one of the country's most important ancient mosques and is traditionally dated to around 1421, although the exact history and date of construction remain debated. The mosque also preserves an exceptionally old Qur'anic manuscript associated with Imam Yidan Barimah Bramah.

That manuscript is particularly striking.

The Ancient Qur'an of Larabanga

The Kamara connection to Islamic antiquity is not preserved only in oral memory.

Larabanga also possesses a physical religious inheritance: an ancient copy of the Qur'an associated with Imam Yidan Barimah Bramah.

The Ghana Museums and Monuments Board records the tradition that the Qur'an was delivered to Imam Bramah around 1650 after he had prayed for one. Other accounts describe the manuscript as being regarded in local tradition as one of the oldest Qur'ans in the world.

The exact age and origin of the manuscript should not be presented as scientifically settled without further examination. But even without making the strongest claim about its age, its significance is difficult to miss.

For generations, an ancient Qur'an has remained connected to the clerical tradition of Larabanga.

It is not simply an old book kept in an old mosque.

It is part of a living Islamic heritage associated with the community and its early clerics.

That gives Larabanga an unusual place in the religious history of Gonja.

The Kamara inheritance is therefore not preserved only in names and oral traditions. It is also attached to a physical religious object that generations have regarded with deep reverence.

The Spiritual Side of Gonja Kingship

The Kamara position also becomes clearer when political authority and spiritual authority are kept separate.

The Yagbonwura is the paramount traditional authority of the Gonja Kingdom.

The Kamara do not claim the skin.

They do not need to.

Their traditional responsibility is different.

The ruler governs the kingdom. The cleric provides religious guidance, prayer and spiritual counsel.

That relationship has continued long after the time of Ndewura Jakpa.

The Yagbonwura's relationship with Larabanga has retained an important spiritual dimension, including the seeking of prayers and blessings from the clerics of the town during significant occasions.

This is consistent with the position the Kamara occupied from the beginning.

Their authority was never supposed to compete with the authority of the king.

It was meant to complement it.

And Where Does That Leave the Sakpari?

It leaves the Sakpari with a place in Gonja history, but not the place they sometimes claim.

Their association with the Gonja chiefs and the royal divisions is real and important.

But service to the royal court is not the same thing as precedence in the history of Gonja's Islamic clerical tradition.

The two should not be confused.

The Kamara were the early clerics who stood with Jakpa.

The Sakpari became associated with the royal establishment.

The first relationship is foundational to the Islamic history surrounding the formation of the kingdom. The second developed within the political and administrative structure of the kingdom.

That is the distinction.

There is no need to insult the Sakpari to make it.

But there is equally no reason to blur it merely to make everyone comfortable.

If the Sakpari's historical importance comes from their relationship with the chiefs, then let that be their history.

It is an honourable history.

But it does not make them the original clerics of the Gonja Kingdom.

Nor does sitting close to the skin automatically place a person above the cleric who guided the man wearing it.

Precedence Cannot Be Rewritten

This is where the present debate needs to be approached honestly.

A later position cannot be turned into an earlier one simply because it became prominent.

The Kamara were already connected to Jakpa.

They were already providing spiritual guidance.

They were already accompanying his warriors.

They were already associated with the religious side of the conquests.

They were later established in Larabanga and Dakurpe.

Their descendants maintained the clerical tradition.

And the relationship between the Kamara and the Gonja traditional institution survived.

These are not claims that depend on a modern title.

They concern the order in which the institutions developed.

That order matters.

The Kamara Place in Gonja

The Kamara therefore do not need to manufacture a place in Gonja history.

Their place is already there.

It is found in the memory of the early conquests.

It is found in the tradition surrounding Kango and the name Kongote.

It is found in Larabanga and Dakurpe.

It is found in the ancient Qur'an preserved in Larabanga.

It is found in the continuing relationship between the Gonja traditional institution and the Kamara clerics.

And, most importantly, it is found at the beginning of the Islamic history associated with Ndewura Jakpa.

The Sakpari have their place.

But their place should not be allowed to swallow the place of the Kamara.

The Gonja Kingdom had its warriors, its chiefs and its clerics. The Kamara belonged to the clerical institution from the earliest period of the kingdom's formation.

That is why the Kamara stand first.

Not because they are asking to be placed there.

Not because a modern dispute has created the claim.

But because that is where their history begins.

And if the history of Gonja is to be told honestly, that beginning must remain where it has always been.
