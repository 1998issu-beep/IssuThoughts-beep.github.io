The True Spiritual Roots of Gonja: Why the Kamara Stand First

The history of Islam in Gonja cannot be properly told without acknowledging the early Kamara clerics who stood alongside Ndewura Jakpa during the formation and expansion of the Gonja Kingdom.

By Ibrahim Yussif • Issu Thoughts

There is an important distinction that must be made whenever the history of Islam in Gonja is discussed: political authority and spiritual authority are not the same thing.

The Gonja Kingdom has its chiefs, its royal divisions and its political institutions. But alongside that political structure existed a clerical tradition that helped give Islam a place within Gonja society. At the beginning of that tradition stands the Kamara.

This is not a recent claim. It is part of the historical tradition preserved in Larabanga, Dakurpe and other communities connected to the early Muslim clerics of Gonja.

Before the Royal Court, There Were the Clerics

The early Kamara presence in Gonja is closely associated with the period of Ndewura Jakpa, the founder of the Gonja Kingdom. According to the historical tradition, Jakpa and his warriors encountered difficulties during their military expansion and sought the assistance of Muslim clerics.

Among those associated with this period was Imam Yidan Braimah, together with other early Kamara clerics. Their role was not simply to stand at the side of the warriors as ordinary members of an expedition. They provided prayers and spiritual guidance and were believed to have protected and supported Jakpa and his men during their campaigns.

The importance of this relationship becomes clearer when the history of the Kamara is placed within the wider expansion of the Gonja Kingdom. The clerics were connected to the very process through which Jakpa and his warriors established Gonja power in territories beyond their original settlements.

The Conquest of Kango and the Name “Kongote”

One of the most remembered episodes concerns the conquest of Kango, in present-day Côte d’Ivoire.

The inherited account holds that Jakpa and his warriors faced particular difficulty in conquering Kango. The spiritual assistance of the Kamara clerics became part of the history surrounding that campaign.

This episode is remembered in the Gonja greeting traditionally associated with the Kamara: “Kongote.” The expression is connected to the idea that Kango had been overcome, and it preserves the memory of the Kamara contribution to that campaign.

That greeting is more than a word of courtesy. It is a reminder of an old relationship between the Gonja political establishment and the Kamara clerical tradition.

Yidan Braimah and the Beginning of the Kamara Position

The historical tradition further connects Imam Yidan Braimah with the establishment of Larabanga. His brother and other members of the wider family became associated with settlements such as Dakurpe, while Yidan continued his association with Jakpa.

This relationship eventually gave the Kamara a distinct position within Gonja society. Their role was not created generations later by a political appointment. It developed from the relationship between the early Muslim clerics and the founder of the Gonja Kingdom.

This is why the position of the Kamara cannot simply be reduced to that of Muslims who arrived in Gonja at a later period. Their place in the historical tradition reaches back to the formation and expansion of the kingdom itself.

Where the Sakpari Fit

This is where the present debate requires some honesty.

The Sakpari have their own place in Gonja history, and that place should not be erased. But having a recognised connection to the royal court does not automatically make a group the original custodians of Gonja's Islamic clerical tradition.

The historical sequence matters.

If the early Kamara clerics were already accompanying Jakpa, providing prayers and spiritual guidance during the expansion of Gonja, then their position precedes the later development of other Muslim relationships around the royal establishment.

That distinction must not be blurred simply because different Muslim families later became important within the kingdom.

The Sakpari can have their place without taking the place of the Kamara.

Royal proximity and clerical precedence are two different things. A family may become closely connected to the palace and still not be the family that introduced, guided or established the earliest Islamic clerical tradition associated with the kingdom.

Larabanga: More Than an Old Mosque

The importance of Larabanga in this history should therefore not be overlooked.

Larabanga is home to the famous ancient mosque that has long been regarded as one of Ghana's most important Islamic heritage sites. The exact date and circumstances of its construction remain subjects of historical discussion, but the mosque's association with the early Muslim tradition of the area is unquestionable.

Larabanga also preserves an ancient Qur'anic manuscript traditionally associated with Imam Yidan Barimah Bramah. Local tradition holds the manuscript in exceptionally high regard and connects it to the spiritual history of the early clerics.

Whether one approaches the manuscript through local tradition, religious memory or historical scholarship, its presence in Larabanga is significant. It represents a physical connection to a much older Islamic heritage that the Kamara have continued to preserve.

A Clerical Tradition That Continued

The Kamara position did not end with the generation of Ndewura Jakpa.

The clerical tradition continued through subsequent generations. Kamara clerics remained associated with Islamic teaching, prayer, mediation and religious affairs within Gonja society.

The relationship between Larabanga and the Gonja kings also remained significant. The traditional association of the Yagbonwura with Larabanga and its clerics before proceeding to Damongo is one example of how the spiritual relationship survived beyond the founding period.

These traditions matter because history is not preserved only in written books. It can also survive through institutions, rituals, greetings, places, manuscripts and relationships that continue across generations.

History Must Respect Its Order

The current argument over Gonja Islamic leadership should therefore not become a contest in which every historical distinction is deliberately blurred.

The question is not whether the Sakpari have a place in Gonja history. They do.

The question is whether their later or parallel relationship with the Gonja royal establishment should be used to rewrite the earlier position of the Kamara clerics.

It should not.

History has an order, and that order matters.

The Kamara were not simply Muslims who appeared somewhere in the margins of Gonja history. Their historical tradition places them alongside Ndewura Jakpa during the formative period of the kingdom. They provided spiritual guidance, participated in the campaigns, and became associated with a distinctive clerical responsibility within Gonja society.

That is the foundation of the Kamara position.

The Place of the Kamara

The Kamara do not need to manufacture a place in Gonja history. Their place is already preserved in Larabanga, Dakurpe, in the traditions of the Gonja people, in the institutions that survived them, and in the memories attached to their relationship with Ndewura Jakpa.

The issue is therefore not about taking something that belongs to another group. It is about refusing to give away something that belongs to the historical record of the Kamara.

The Sakpari have their history. The Kamara have theirs.

But the two histories should not be merged simply to create an easier narrative.

If the question is who stands at the beginning of the Gonja Islamic clerical tradition associated with Ndewura Jakpa, the Kamara position is clear: they stand there.

That is not an insult to the Sakpari. It is simply the distinction that history requires.

And when the history of Islam in Gonja is written, the Kamara should not appear as an afterthought. They belong near the beginning of the chapter.
